I've watched designers spend two weeks on a prototype that could have been a sketch on paper. I've also watched teams ship a product based on a prototype that didn't actually test what they thought it did.

The common thread is a misunderstanding of what a prototype is for.

## A prototype answers a question

Before I open Figma, I write the question down. Literally. One sentence on a sticky note.

*Does this navigation model make sense to a first-time user?*

*Can users complete the onboarding flow without asking for help?*

*Does this interaction feel fast enough to be satisfying?*

The question determines everything: fidelity, scope, who to test with, and what to measure. If the question is about information architecture, a clickable wireframe is enough. If it's about perceived performance, you might need to prototype in code.

## Fidelity is a variable, not a quality signal

High fidelity is not better than low fidelity. It's appropriate for different questions.

Low-fidelity prototypes — sketches, paper mockups, simple click-throughs — are fast to make and easy to throw away. They signal to participants that this isn't precious, which makes their feedback more honest.

High-fidelity prototypes are worth the investment when the question involves visual design, microinteractions, or emotional response. Not before.

The mistake is treating high fidelity as a default. It slows you down and creates attachment — in you and in stakeholders — that makes honest evaluation harder.

## What you're actually testing

When I run a prototype session, I'm not testing the prototype. I'm testing the design decisions embedded in it. That's a subtle but important distinction.

If users struggle with a task, I want to know *why*. Is the label ambiguous? Is the affordance unclear? Is the mental model mismatched? The prototype is a vehicle for discovering which assumptions were wrong.

That's why I always debrief after tasks: "what were you expecting to happen?" and "what would have helped you here?" The behaviour tells me what failed. The debrief tells me why.

## Iterate before you refine

The best thing about a prototype is that you can change it. I try to run at least three rounds of testing before I move to development handoff — not because I distrust my designs, but because each round teaches me something I couldn't have predicted.

The goal isn't a prototype with zero problems. It's a design where the problems that remain are ones you've chosen to accept, not ones you haven't discovered yet.
