There's a temptation in digital product design to add. Add a feature for this edge case. Add a tooltip to explain that confusing label. Add an option so the user can decide.

Every addition feels helpful in isolation. Together, they create a product that exhausts the people using it.

## Complexity is a symptom

When I encounter a complex interface, I've learned to read it as a signal — usually that something upstream is unresolved. Unclear ownership between teams. A brief that wasn't specific enough. A stakeholder who wanted options instead of a recommendation.

The designer's job, a lot of the time, is to go back upstream and resolve that ambiguity. Not to design around it.

## The clarity test

I run every major design decision through a simple test: can I explain what this screen is for in one sentence? If the answer requires "it depends" or a list of scenarios, the screen is doing too much.

This doesn't mean every screen needs to do only one thing. It means every screen needs a clear primary purpose, and everything else on it should serve that purpose or get out of the way.

## Reduction as a design act

Cutting is designing. When I remove a secondary action from a key flow, I'm making a claim: this doesn't belong here, and users are better off without it in this moment.

That requires conviction, which requires understanding — of the user, the business goal, and the context of use. Reduction without that understanding is just minimalism for aesthetics.

## What it looks like in practice

On a recent project, the client had a checkout flow with eleven steps. They wanted to reduce it to seven. I pushed back: the goal shouldn't be "fewer steps" — it should be "fewer decisions." We restructured the flow around that principle and got to four steps, which felt effortless to test users even though they were doing the same work.

The lesson: clarity isn't about how little you show. It's about how easily users can understand what they're looking at and what to do next.
