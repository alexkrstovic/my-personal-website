Good design doesn't start with sketches or wireframes. It starts with a question: *who is this for, and what do they actually need?*

Over the years I've refined a research process that keeps me grounded in real user needs rather than assumptions. Here's how it works.

## Starting with discovery

Before I talk to a single user, I spend time understanding the problem space. That means reviewing any existing analytics, reading competitor reviews, and interviewing stakeholders to surface their assumptions — because assumptions are what I'm there to challenge.

The goal of discovery isn't to find answers. It's to write better questions.

## The interview

I run semi-structured interviews. I prepare a guide of about eight to ten questions, but I treat it as a map rather than a script. The most valuable moments usually come from following a tangent the participant introduces.

A few rules I hold firmly:

- Never ask "would you use this?" — people lie when they imagine future behaviour
- Ask about the last time they did something, not what they usually do
- Silence is useful; let it breathe

I record everything with permission and take sparse notes during the session so I can stay present.

## Synthesis

This is the messiest and most important part. I transcribe key quotes, pull out observations, and run an affinity mapping session — usually with the team, since collective interpretation reduces my own bias.

From the clusters I look for:
1. **Jobs to be done** — what are people trying to accomplish?
2. **Pain points** — where does the current experience fail them?
3. **Mental models** — how do they think about the problem, and does our product match that model?

## From insight to decision

Research only matters if it changes something. I write a one-page summary for every project — findings, implications, and recommended directions — and present it before any design work begins. If the findings contradict the project brief, that conversation needs to happen early.

The best outcome of a research session isn't a satisfied client. It's a team that's learned something they didn't know they didn't know.
